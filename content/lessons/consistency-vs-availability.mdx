Your app has two database servers — a primary that handles writes, and a replica that handles reads. You did this to reduce load on the primary.

A user updates their username. The write goes to the primary. Half a second later, the same user loads their profile. The read goes to the replica.

The replica hasn't synced yet. They see their old username.

They refresh. It's correct. They're confused. You have a consistency problem.

## The CAP Theorem

The CAP theorem says a distributed system can only guarantee two of three properties at any time when a **network partition** occurs (when nodes can't communicate):

- **Consistency** — every read returns the most recent write
- **Availability** — every request gets a response (not an error)
- **Partition Tolerance** — the system keeps working even when some nodes can't talk to each other

Network partitions happen. You can't opt out. So the real choice is always **CP vs AP**.

Step 1: You have two database nodes. Step 2: A write goes to the primary. The read goes to the replica. Step 3: The replica hasn't synced yet — the client sees stale data. That's eventual consistency.

## CP Systems: Consistent but May Refuse

A CP system chooses consistency over availability. When partitioned, it refuses to respond rather than return potentially stale data.

**Example:** A bank transfer. You can't have two ATMs both thinking there's $500 in an account when one withdrawal hasn't replicated yet. The system refuses the second withdrawal until it's sure about the balance.

CP databases: Postgres with synchronous replication, HBase, Zookeeper.

## AP Systems: Available but May Be Stale

An AP system chooses availability over consistency. When partitioned, it keeps responding — but some responses might be stale. Data converges to consistency eventually.

**Example:** Your Twitter-style like counter. If it's off by 3 for half a second, nobody cares. The app stays up, users can interact, and the counts sync eventually.

AP databases: Cassandra, DynamoDB (default), CouchDB.

## Eventual Consistency in Practice

Most user-facing web apps are AP with **eventual consistency**: after a write, all nodes will eventually agree — just not instantly.

The username display bug from the opening story is a form of eventual consistency. The fix options are:

1. **Read your own writes** — after a write, route subsequent reads from the same user to the primary for a short window
2. **Synchronous replication** — the write doesn't complete until the replica confirms it (slower writes)
3. **Accept it** — a half-second lag on username display is genuinely acceptable for most products

## The Practical Takeaway

Most apps should run Postgres with a single primary. You don't encounter these trade-offs until you add replicas or split across data centers.

When you do, the question is: for each type of data in your system, how stale is too stale? Financial data: zero tolerance. Social feed: seconds are fine. Profile data: under a second is usually acceptable.

That tolerance guides your replication and caching strategy.
