System design is about making trade-offs. These principles help you make them deliberately.

## Reliability

The system works correctly even when things go wrong. Servers crash. Networks fail. Disks corrupt. A reliable system handles these gracefully.

**How:** Replication, redundancy, retries with backoff, graceful degradation.

## Scalability

The system continues to work as load increases. What works at 100 users should work at 1,000,000.

**How:** Horizontal scaling, caching, async processing, database sharding.

## Maintainability

Other engineers can understand, modify, and fix the system. This matters more than you think — most time in software is spent reading code, not writing it.

**How:** Clear boundaries between components, good observability, simple over clever.

## The CAP Theorem

A distributed system can only guarantee two of three properties:

- **Consistency** — every read returns the most recent write
- **Availability** — every request receives a response
- **Partition tolerance** — the system works even when parts can't communicate

You must choose. Most real systems choose AP (available + partition tolerant) and accept eventual consistency.

## Design for Failure

Assume every component will fail. Design your system to fail gracefully rather than catastrophically.

A circuit breaker stops calling a failing downstream service and returns a default response. A retry with exponential backoff handles transient failures. A timeout ensures you don't wait forever.

## Observability First

You can't fix what you can't see. Add structured logging, metrics, and tracing from day one. When something breaks at 3am, these are your only tools.
