A new engineer joins your team. You want to explain how your app works.

You could start with the code — "so in `app/api/posts/route.ts` we have..." — but 20 minutes in you're both lost in details and they still don't understand the big picture.

Or you could draw a diagram in 5 minutes that shows: "here's the browser, here's our API, here's the database, here's Redis for caching, here's our email service." Everything clicks.

Architecture diagrams are the shared language of system design. This lesson teaches you to read and draw them.

## What a Good Diagram Shows

A diagram answers three questions:
1. What components exist?
2. How do they communicate?
3. Where are the boundaries?

It does not need to show every function, every file, or every edge case. It needs to communicate the structure at a useful level of detail.

## The Common Symbols

No universal standard, but these are widely understood:

- **Rectangle** — a service, server, or component
- **Cylinder** — a database (Postgres, MySQL, SQLite)
- **Dashed rectangle** — a boundary or logical group (e.g., "runs in the cloud")
- **Arrow** — communication, labeled with protocol: `HTTP/JSON`, `TCP`, `Redis RESP`
- **Cloud shape** — an external system or the internet

Label your arrows. An arrow without a label is ambiguous — does this call happen synchronously? Is it an event? What protocol?

## Zoom Levels

The C4 model provides a useful framework for choosing the right level of detail:

**System Context** — your system as a black box, surrounded by users and external systems. "This is what our app does and what it talks to."

**Container** — the major technical pieces: web app, API, database, Redis, workers. "This is how our app is built."

**Component** — the internals of one container: the auth module, the billing service, the recommendations engine. "This is how this part works."

Start at the Container level. It's usually the most useful for understanding a system quickly.

## The Practical Rules

**Draw the simplest diagram that answers the question you're trying to answer.** If you're deciding whether to add a cache, draw the current data flow and where the cache would sit. Don't include the CDN or the workers — they're not relevant to the decision.

**Keep diagrams in your repo.** Diagrams stored in Notion or Confluence drift from the code and become wrong. A `docs/architecture.md` with an embedded Mermaid diagram stays next to the code and gets updated when the code changes.

**Use tools you'll actually use.** Excalidraw for quick sketches, Mermaid for version-controlled diagrams in markdown, draw.io for detailed documentation.

The goal isn't a perfect diagram. It's a shared mental model.
